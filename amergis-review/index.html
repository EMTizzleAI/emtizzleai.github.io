01 / My experience

Professional people deserve professional treatment.

I accepted school-based work through Amergis with the expectation that the agency would understand my qualifications, communicate directly, and respect the time required to show up for students. My experience fell far short of that standard.

01 —

My experience should have mattered.

I brought a background in emergency medical services and work supporting people with behavioral and educational needs. I did not feel my résumé, qualifications, or perspective were taken seriously.

02 —

Onboarding was exhausting.

What should have been a straightforward process felt unnecessarily prolonged and disorganized. There were repeated communications and administrative frustrations. It felt like it took five years just to get onboarded.

03 —

The confusion cost me time and peace of mind.

Every unclear instruction and avoidable delay has a real cost for someone trying to work, plan their schedule, and earn a living. I felt my time and financial realities were disregarded.

04 —

Then came the 7:30 a.m. call.

On October 26, 2026, shortly before a scheduled school assignment, I received a call saying the agency was not comfortable having me report. I asked for a clear explanation. The conversation left me without the straightforward answers I needed about that assignment.

“If your business depends on practitioners showing up for people, you cannot treat those practitioners as expendable.”
My view as a school-based practitioner and EMS professional
02 / A bigger problem

Respect autistic practitioners, too.

I am an autistic professional. It is especially painful to deal with an organization that places people in autism- and behavioral-support roles while feeling misunderstood, scrutinized, and dismissed in my own professional interactions. My criticism is of how I was treated—not of the students, teachers, or other frontline staff doing the work.

↗

Read the résumé.

Recognize relevant experience before treating a qualified practitioner like an unknown quantity.

↗

Communicate plainly.

Confirm assignments, changes, and employment decisions directly—and in writing.

↗

Honor people's time.

Last-minute changes are not abstract scheduling problems. They affect real people and their livelihoods.

↗

Practice the inclusion you advertise.

Neurodivergent staff deserve the same dignity and professional respect that an education staffing company says it values.

03 / Before you sign on

Ask for the details in writing.

My experience is my own. If you are considering an Amergis assignment, protect your time and evaluate the arrangement for yourself.

  • Is my assignment confirmed, including location and start time?
  • Who makes placement and cancellation decisions?
  • What happens if a shift is cancelled at the last minute?
  • How is my time recorded and approved?
  • Who will clarify my employment status in writing?
04 / My conclusion

I wouldn't recommend Amergis.

I felt disrespected, poorly communicated with, and let down by an agency that should know better. Healthcare and education workers have choices. I would make a different one.
